# Weather references: candidate photographs

Candidate reference photographs for the nine required pictures of [the rubric](mvp.md) §2, gathered for the orchestrator to freeze (§3.1). Geometry for each picture is from [phase 1's design](phase1/design.md) §3.3. The images live in `.data/weather/references/candidates/<id>/` and are not committed.

Every candidate below was downloaded and looked at. Long edges are about 2,000 px where the source allows: NASA frames are reduced from the full JPEG to 2048 px with `sips`; Commons files are Commons' own 1920 px rendering, or the original where it is smaller; EPIC frames are the 2048 px PNG converted to JPEG. The SHA-256 is of the local file as saved. Licenses were read from each file's own page: the NASA Gateway's terms page for ISS photographs, the EPIC about page for EPIC, and each Commons file page's license field and template. Every candidate has a clear license; nothing is listed separately for an unclear one.

Columns: **File** is the local path under `candidates/`; **Capture** is what the source records (times are UTC for NASA, the camera clock for Commons); **Shows / misleads** is what a render should match, then what in the photograph could pull a judge the wrong way.

  116–400 mm against the preset's 15°, so they judge color and layering, not
  the band's size on screen.

  sub-observer point. A December disk has a sunlit Antarctica and a bright
  limb.

  stages.

## E1 — Cumulus from orbit

The preset looks straight down from 400 km over the Amazon basin (5° S 60° W) with a 120.2 mm-equivalent lens: an 11.4° vertical field, about 80 km top to bottom, the sun 50° up and falling. Every candidate is an ISS photograph from the Gateway to Astronaut Photography, taken on a full-frame Nikon (36 × 24 mm), so the focal length converts directly; footprints below are nadir approximations. The database's search form was used with near-vertical tilt, 25–75 % cloud, 100–500 mm and sun 35–70° over tropical South America and the Congo, then screened by eye.

## E2 — The limb at dusk

The preset looks from 400 km toward the sunset limb at pitch −19.8° with a 91.1 mm-equivalent lens (15° vertical), the sun 22° under the horizon beneath the eye. The database records the sun's elevation at the nadir, and seven of the eight candidates sit between −20° and −25°, so the preset's −22° is the ordinary geometry of an ISS dusk limb photograph. Candidates come from Commons' 'Sunsets from the International Space Station' category and Earth Observatory captions, mapped back to their database IDs.

DVIDS was not indexed by the search. The set therefore has one stratus descent (four frames) and two views of cumulus from just outside. Someone fetching NOAA's collection by hand is the likeliest way to close the gap.
- **G4, a cloudbow on a deck seen from an aircraft.** One example was found (`g4/glory-cloudbow-deck.jpg`), and it is heavily color-saturated. Fogbows from the ground are plentiful on Commons but are the wrong geometry.
- **G2 at full size.** Full overcast over open water is common but rarely photographed for its own sake; three of the eight candidates are geograph.org.uk imports at 1024 px.
- **E1 at the preset's field.** The database's near-vertical cloud photographs over tropical land cluster at 180–400 mm; only two candidates (110 and 140 mm) approach the preset's 120 mm-equivalent field, and both are tilted more than 20°.
- **The Gateway's JSON API** needs a key requested by email, so it was not used; the site's own technical search form (`Technical.pl`) did the same job without one.

## What the photographs suggest about the definitions

1. **E1's lens.** The brief asks for 400 mm or longer, but the preset's 11.4° vertical field is a 120 mm lens on the full-frame Nikons ISS crews use. A 400 mm frame covers about 37 × 25 km, a third of the preset's 80 km height, so its cells look three times larger on screen than the render's. The cells themselves agree with the rubric: in every frame they are about one to three kilometers across, and at a 50° sun (`iss061-e-59841`, `iss064-e-6210`) each shadow sits roughly a cell's width from its cloud, as design §3.3 derives for a 1.2 km base. Prefer the 110–210 mm frames for form, and tell the seats the scale of any 400 mm frame in the packet.
2. **E2's sun is right; its lens is not what the references show.** Seven ISS dusk limb photographs record the sun 20–25° under the nadir horizon, so −22° is the ordinary case. But they are shot at 240–460 mm, and at those lenses the band fills a large part of the frame; at the preset's 15° it is the 150 CSS px design §3.3 derives. The references can judge color, layering and cloud silhouettes, not the band's size on screen. Separately, "the shadows they lay on the air" is barely visible in any −20° frame; long cloud shadows show clearly only near the terminator (`iss060-e-71360`, sun −4°). That rubric item may be weaker evidence than it reads.
3. **E3's face.** December EPIC frames center near 25° S, with Antarctica large, sunlit and the brightest thing on the disk, and a bright hazy limb. A J2000 render that does not show a lit Antarctica and a brightened limb will read as wrong beside them. If `blue-marble`'s framing does not face the subsolar point, only the frames whose centroid matches the framing's sub-observer point are fair comparisons; the 2017-01-01 and 2017-12-20 frames are nearest the J2000 subsolar longitude.
4. **G1's ground shadows may be unscorable from the preset's stance.** In eye-level photographs over flat ground, cloud shadows on the ground are barely visible; the only candidate that shows them clearly (`g1/shadows-bald-eagle-forest.jpg`) is taken from a vista above a valley. A 2 m eye at pitch 12° over flat ground will show little, so either the Shadow dimension for G1 is scored on the bases' shading, or the stance moves to a rise.
5. **G2's reflection.** Under a real overcast the sea is gray-green and usually darker than the sky, and the deck's texture appears in the water only where it is calm (`oso-bay-overcast`, `st-ives-harbor`). "The deck in the sea" holds for calm water; a wind-roughened sea will show a flat gray.
6. **G3 sits near the end of the light.** The underlit photographs are taken from sunset to about five minutes after, when the sun is roughly 0–1° under the horizon. At −2° a layer at 4.0–4.8 km is still lit (on Earth until about −2.1° plus refraction; on the 3,980 km generated world until about −2.7°), but only by light that has crossed a long path of low air, so it is deep red and dimming fast. A small error in the sun's angle or the layer's height flips the picture between lit and unlit; the references show the lit state.
7. **G4 has no aircraft.** Every aircraft glory is centered on the aircraft's shadow, and judges will look for one. From 3 km an airliner's shadow is mostly penumbra but still visible; the preset has no body at the eye, so its glory has an empty center. The packet's brief should say so. The cloudbow is rare in photographs, so the seats have only one, saturated, reference for it.
8. **G5's reference does not exist as specified.** The rubric names cockpit video; what was found is a descent through stratus. It shows visibility collapsing and returning, but in the wrong genus. Either G5 is judged on the continuity specification and the measured visibility range, with these frames as illustration, or someone retrieves NOAA's cockpit series by hand.
9. **G6 is judged against §4, not photographs.** The candidates illustrate each stage of the descent: orbit, cruise, below the base and the ground.
